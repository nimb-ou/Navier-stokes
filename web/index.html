Historical Perspective

The 90-Year Quest: How We Used to Think

Since Claude-Louis Navier (1822) and George Gabriel Stokes (1845) formulated the viscous fluid equations, mathematicians held a deep intuitive belief: viscosity diffuses and smooths out velocity gradients. Whenever velocity gradients $\nabla u$ become large, the viscous dissipation term $\nu \Delta u$ acts as $\sim \nu k^2$ on spatial frequency $k$, suppressing high-frequency oscillations faster than non-linear advection $(u \cdot \nabla)u$ can create them.

What We Believed (1934–2025)

Viscosity prevents singularities by dissipating kinetic energy at small scales. Any high-shear vortex filament will thicken and diffuse outward before velocities can diverge, ensuring solutions starting from smooth data remain smooth forever.

What Was Proven (OpenAI 2026)

An anisotropic vortex column can contract faster radially ($\ell_r \sim \tau^{1/2}$) than axially ($\ell_z \sim \tau^{1/2-h}$), creating a razor-thin needle. High-frequency shear-amplified wave packets in the annulus cancel the momentum imbalance, allowing velocity to diverge to infinity while total energy stays bounded.

1934
Jean Leray Constructs Global Weak Solutions
Leray proves that global weak solutions with finite kinetic energy always exist, but cannot prove they remain smooth. He famously conjectures that turbulent singularities might occur when vortex filaments concentrate.
1982
Caffarelli, Kohn, and Nirenberg (CKN Theorem)
Proves that the singular set of suitable weak solutions has parabolic 1D Hausdorff measure zero. If a singularity exists, it can only occur at isolated points in space and time!
2000
The Clay Mathematics Institute Millennium Prize
Charles Fefferman states the $1,000,000 problem. He explicitly poses alternative (A) [global existence on $\mathbb{R}^3$ without force], (B) [periodic without force], and breakdown alternatives (C) [breakdown on $\mathbb{R}^3$ with smooth compact force] and (D) [breakdown on $\mathbb{T}^3$].
2016
Terence Tao’s Averaged Navier–Stokes Blowup
Tao constructs finite-time blowup for an averaged version of Navier–Stokes with the exact same energy identities, proving that basic energy cancellation alone is fundamentally insufficient to guarantee regularity.
2019
Buckmaster and Vicol: Convex Integration
Proves non-uniqueness of finite-energy weak solutions for 3D Navier–Stokes using convex integration methods originally pioneered by Nash and De Lellis–Székelyhidi.
2026
OpenAI Formalizes Finite-Time Blowup in Lean 4
Establishes alternatives (C) and (D) in Fefferman’s Millennium problem statement! Constructs smooth initial data starting from rest and a smooth compactly supported force leading to finite-time singularity with bounded kinetic energy, machine-verified in Lean 4 without gaps.
Mathematical Mechanism

The Anatomy of Blowup: How It Breaks the Equation

The breakthrough rests on three interlocking physical ideas: an anisotropic self-similar core, an exact incompressibility balance with inward spiraling and dual axial jets, and the bounded energy paradox.

1. The Anisotropic Vortex Needle

The fluid coordinates are scaled by two different powers of $\tau = 1 - t$: $$ \ell_r \asymp \tau^{1/2}, \quad \ell_z \asymp \tau^{1/2 - h} \quad (0 < h < 1/100) $$ Because $h > 0$, the radius $\ell_r$ shrinks much faster than the axial height $\ell_z$. The aspect ratio $\ell_r / \ell_z \asymp \tau^h \to 0$ collapses the flow into an infinitely sharp needle along the $z$-axis.

2. The Inward Spiral & Axial Jet Topology

In cylindrical coordinates $(r, \theta, z)$, incompressibility requires: $$ \frac{1}{r}\partial_r(r u_r) + \partial_z u_z = 0 $$ Radial inflow ($u_r < 0$) carries angular momentum $r u_\theta$ inward to smaller radii, spinning up the vortex. Viscosity diffuses angular momentum outward. The fluid cannot accumulate at $r = 0$, so it is continuously evacuated by the axial outflow $u_z$, shooting upwards for $z > 0$ and downwards for $z < 0$.

To prevent reflection symmetry from killing the axial shear at $z = 0$, a small upward velocity offset $j_0 > 0$ is added, ensuring robust amplification everywhere.

Convex Integration & Wave Mechanics

The Annular Wave Packets: Canceling the Momentum Residual

If one simply splices this self-similar vortex core onto an exterior heat flow, the Navier–Stokes momentum residual: $$ \mathcal{R}(u, p) = \partial_t u + (u \cdot \nabla)u - \nu \Delta u + \nabla p $$ diverges as $t \uparrow 1$ in the annulus between the core and exterior ($X_a < X < X_b$).

To cancel this singular residual, the paper injects high-frequency oscillatory wave packets $w$: $$ \mathcal{R}(u_B + w, p_B + \pi) = \mathcal{R}(u_B, p_B) + \mathcal{L}_{u_B}(w, \pi) + \nabla \cdot (w \otimes w) $$

The nonlinear Reynolds stress tensor $\langle w \otimes w \rangle$ has two wave families ($\sigma = \pm 1$) that extract kinetic energy from the background shear. Viscous damping increases as the wave orientation shortens the radial wavelength, causing the wave envelope $P(v)$ to grow and then decay smoothly. The divergence of this stress precisely cancels the singular residual, leaving a smooth external force $f$!
